Understanding Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models

V
Vineet Kumar
Applied Scientist II
TL;DR
A diffusion model destroys images on purpose, a little Gaussian noise at a time, and trains a network to undo one step of that damage. Once the network is good at it, you hand it pure noise and let it clean up for 1000 steps, and a brand-new image comes out. The loss looks scary on paper, but after some algebra it turns into a plain mean squared error between the noise you added and the noise the network thinks you added. I implemented it on MNIST, and the results are at the end.

You may already know about a subfield of machine learning called generative modeling. The goal is to learn the distribution the data came from, and then draw new samples from that learned distribution. That's how we get image generators like Stable Diffusion, DALL·E 2 and Imagen, along with synthetic training data, image inpainting and super-resolution, speech and music generation, protein and molecule design, and, less happily, deepfakes.

There are several ways to build such a model: GANs, variational autoencoders (the encoder-decoder family), normalizing flows, autoregressive models, and diffusion models. In this post I'll go through one of them in detail, Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models, or DDPM, from Ho, Jain and Abbeel (2020).

The idea: learn to remove noise

Put simply, a DDPM learns to remove noise from a sample. Take an image and add a small amount of Gaussian noise to it. Then add a bit more, and keep going for hundreds of small steps (the paper uses T=1000T = 1000T=1000) until nothing of the image is left and you're looking at pure static. This is called the forward process, and it is fixed in advance, so there is nothing in it to learn.

Each step of the forward process looks only at the image from the step before it:

q(xt∣xt−1)=N ⁣(xt; 1−βt xt−1, βtI)q(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_{t-1}) = \mathcal{N}\!\left(\mathbf{x}_t;\ \sqrt{1-\beta_t}\,\mathbf{x}_{t-1},\ \beta_t \mathbf{I}\right)q(xt​∣xt−1​)=N(xt​; 1−βt​​xt−1​, βt​I)

Because each step depends only on the previous one, the forward process is a Markov chain. βt\beta_tβt​ is a small number that sets how much noise goes in at step ttt. I'll come back to how it's chosen.

The model's job is to run this chain backwards. Given a noisy image xt\mathbf{x}_txt​, it has to produce a slightly cleaner xt−1\mathbf{x}_{t-1}xt−1​. That learned reverse step is pθ(xt−1∣xt)p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_t)pθ​(xt−1​∣xt​), and the paper draws the whole picture like this:

One property makes training cheap. Since every step only adds Gaussian noise, you don't have to run 500 steps to get x500\mathbf{x}_{500}x500​. You can jump straight to any ttt in one go:

xt=αˉt x0+1−αˉt ϵ,ϵ∼N(0,I)αt=1−βt,αˉt=∏s=1tαs\begin{gathered} \mathbf{x}_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\mathbf{x}_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\boldsymbol\epsilon, \qquad \boldsymbol\epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I}) \\ \alpha_t = 1-\beta_t, \qquad \bar\alpha_t = \prod_{s=1}^{t} \alpha_s \end{gathered}xt​=αˉt​​x0​+1−αˉt​​ϵ,ϵ∼N(0,I)αt​=1−βt​,αˉt​=s=1∏t​αs​​

So αˉt\bar\alpha_tαˉt​ says how much of the original image survives at step ttt, and 1−αˉt1 - \bar\alpha_t1−αˉt​ says how much of it is noise. That's the number under each frame in Fig 1.

What should the network predict?

There are a few equivalent ways to set up the network's output, and the paper discusses three of them (Section 3.2):

  1. Predict the noise ϵ\boldsymbol\epsilonϵ that was mixed into x0\mathbf{x}_0x0​ to get xt\mathbf{x}_txt​. This is what DDPM ends up using. Note that the target is the total noise from the formula above, not the difference between xt\mathbf{x}_txt​ and xt−1\mathbf{x}_{t-1}xt−1​.
  2. Predict the clean image x0\mathbf{x}_0x0​ directly. The paper tried this and got worse samples early in their experiments.
  3. Predict the mean of xt−1\mathbf{x}_{t-1}xt−1​, i.e. where the previous, less noisy image should be on average. This works about as well as predicting ϵ\boldsymbol\epsilonϵ when trained on the full loss, but predicting ϵ\boldsymbol\epsilonϵ does much better once you switch to the simplified loss we'll get to below.

Given xt\mathbf{x}_txt​ and ttt, any one of these lets you compute the other two. The choice affects how hard the target is to learn and how the loss weights different timesteps, and predicting the noise turned out to work best.

Sampling is the forward process run in reverse. Start from pure noise xT∼N(0,I)\mathbf{x}_T \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})xT​∼N(0,I) and call the network 1000 times, removing a bit of predicted noise each time. What comes out at the end is a new sample from (approximately) the data distribution:

The loss

So how do you train a network for this? Like VAEs, DDPM is trained by minimizing an upper bound on the negative log-likelihood of the data (the variational bound):

E[−log⁡pθ(x0)]≤Eq ⁣[−log⁡pθ(x0:T)q(x1:T∣x0)]=Eq ⁣[−log⁡p(xT)−∑t≥1log⁡pθ(xt−1∣xt)q(xt∣xt−1)]=:L\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}\left[-\log p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_0)\right] &\le \mathbb{E}_q\!\left[-\log \frac{p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_{0:T})}{q(\mathbf{x}_{1:T} \mid \mathbf{x}_0)}\right] \\ &= \mathbb{E}_q\!\left[-\log p(\mathbf{x}_T) - \sum_{t \ge 1} \log \frac{p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_t)}{q(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_{t-1})}\right] =: L \end{aligned}E[−logpθ​(x0​)]​≤Eq​[−logq(x1:T​∣x0​)pθ​(x0:T​)​]=Eq​[−logp(xT​)−t≥1∑​logq(xt​∣xt−1​)pθ​(xt−1​∣xt​)​]=:L​

After some rearranging, it splits into three kinds of terms:

L=Eq[DKL(q(xT∣x0) ∥ p(xT))⏟LT+∑t>1DKL(q(xt−1∣xt,x0) ∥ pθ(xt−1∣xt))⏟Lt−1−log⁡pθ(x0∣x1)⏟L0]\begin{aligned} L = \mathbb{E}_q\Big[ &\underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(\mathbf{x}_T \mid \mathbf{x}_0)\,\|\,p(\mathbf{x}_T)\big)}_{L_T} \\ &+ \sum_{t>1} \underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{x}_0)\,\|\,p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_t)\big)}_{L_{t-1}} \\ &\underbrace{-\log p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_0 \mid \mathbf{x}_1)}_{L_0}\Big] \end{aligned}L=Eq​[​LT​DKL​(q(xT​∣x0​)∥p(xT​))​​+t>1∑​Lt−1​DKL​(q(xt−1​∣xt​,x0​)∥pθ​(xt−1​∣xt​))​​L0​−logpθ​(x0​∣x1​)​​]​

Each term has a different job, and here's what they mean, as intuitively as I can put it:

  1. LTL_TLT​: did the forward process end in noise? It compares where the forward process lands after TTT steps, q(xT∣x0)q(\mathbf{x}_T \mid \mathbf{x}_0)q(xT​∣x0​), with the pure noise N(0,I)\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})N(0,I) that sampling starts from. If the two match, starting the reverse process from random noise is justified. The βt\beta_tβt​ are fixed, so this term has no learnable parameters. It's a constant, and the training code ignores it. With the paper's schedule it's almost exactly zero.
  2. Lt−1L_{t-1}Lt−1​: does the model's denoising step match the true one? This is where the learning happens. If you know the clean image x0\mathbf{x}_0x0​, the true reverse step q(xt−1∣xt,x0)q(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{x}_0)q(xt−1​∣xt​,x0​) can be computed exactly, and it's a Gaussian. The model doesn't get to see x0\mathbf{x}_0x0​, so its step pθ(xt−1∣xt)p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_{t-1} \mid \mathbf{x}_t)pθ​(xt−1​∣xt​) has to guess. Both are Gaussians with fixed variances, so the KL between them reduces to the squared distance between their means. Write the mean in terms of the noise and that distance becomes "how far is the predicted noise from the actual noise", which is why the network ends up predicting ϵ\boldsymbol\epsilonϵ.
  3. L0L_0L0​: the reconstruction term. It's the very last step, from x1\mathbf{x}_1x1​ to the actual image, and it measures how likely the true pixel values are under the model's final output. It's the same role the reconstruction loss plays in a VAE.

The paper then makes a practical simplification to this loss. Written out, Lt−1L_{t-1}Lt−1​ carries a weight in front of each timestep. Ho et al. drop those weights and train on the plain version:

Lsimple=Et, x0, ϵ[∥ϵ−ϵθ ⁣(αˉt x0+1−αˉt ϵ, t)∥2]L_{\text{simple}} = \mathbb{E}_{t,\,\mathbf{x}_0,\,\boldsymbol\epsilon}\left[\left\|\boldsymbol\epsilon - \boldsymbol\epsilon_\theta\!\left(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\mathbf{x}_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\boldsymbol\epsilon,\ t\right)\right\|^2\right]Lsimple​=Et,x0​,ϵ​[​ϵ−ϵθ​(αˉt​​x0​+1−αˉt​​ϵ, t)​2]

That's the whole training objective: mix noise into a clean image, ask the network what noise you mixed in, and penalize the squared error. Dropping the weights puts less emphasis on the easy, barely noisy timesteps and more on the hard, very noisy ones, and the paper found that this gave better samples. The fresh noise is skipped on the very last step, because the output of that step should be the clean image.

The noise schedule

A couple of questions you might have: what are αt\alpha_tαt​ and βt\beta_tβt​, and why the square roots? βt\beta_tβt​ is the noise schedule. The goal is to make xT\mathbf{x}_TxT​ a standard Gaussian, with mean 0 and variance 1, because that's the distribution we sample from when generating.

The mean takes care of itself. The noise has zero mean, and each step multiplies the image by 1−βt<1\sqrt{1-\beta_t} < 11−βt​​<1, so after enough steps the original signal is scaled down to almost nothing and the mean goes to zero. The variance needs more care, and that's what the square roots are for. If xt−1\mathbf{x}_{t-1}xt−1​ has unit variance, then

Var⁡(xt)=(1−βt)⋅1+βt=1\operatorname{Var}(\mathbf{x}_t) = (1-\beta_t)\cdot 1 + \beta_t = 1Var(xt​)=(1−βt​)⋅1+βt​=1

so the variance stays at 1 the whole way through instead of growing with every step. The paper sets βt\beta_tβt​ to rise linearly from 10−410^{-4}10−4 to 0.020.020.02 over T=1000T = 1000T=1000 steps. That leaves αˉT≈4×10−5\bar\alpha_T \approx 4 \times 10^{-5}αˉT​≈4×10−5, so xT\mathbf{x}_TxT​ is about as close to pure noise as you can get. Keeping each βt\beta_tβt​ small also matters for the reverse direction: when a step adds only a little noise, undoing it is well approximated by a Gaussian, which is the form the model's pθp_\thetapθ​ assumes.

My implementation on MNIST

Everything else is implementation detail, and you can find my implementation on MNIST here. It's a small UNet with two downsampling levels and a sinusoidal time embedding, trained with the paper's linear schedule and T=1000T = 1000T=1000, batch size 64 and Adam at a learning rate of 10−410^{-4}10−4. Each epoch takes about a minute on a MacBook GPU. Here's what 16 random samples looked like as training went on:

At epoch 0 you get scribbles with roughly the right stroke width. By epoch 5 the strokes are thick and blobby, and from there they get thinner and cleaner. By epoch 30 most of the digits are readable, though a few are still malformed. What surprised me was the training loss:

The loss is almost flat after epoch 5 (it goes from 0.027 to 0.024), yet the samples keep getting noticeably better. The noise-prediction loss is a bad guide to sample quality, so look at samples to decide when to stop. The paper also keeps an exponential moving average of the weights for sampling, which my version doesn't do yet, and that should clean up the remaining odd digits.

Key takeaways
  1. The forward process is fixed: it adds a little Gaussian noise per step until the image is pure noise, and you can jump to any step in closed form.
  2. The network learns the reverse step by predicting the noise that was added, trained with a plain mean squared error.
  3. The variational bound splits into a constant (LTL_TLT​), per-step denoising terms (Lt−1L_{t-1}Lt−1​) and a reconstruction term (L0L_0L0​). The per-step terms are where the learning happens.
  4. The 1−βt\sqrt{1-\beta_t}1−βt​​ scaling keeps the variance at 1, so after enough steps the image becomes a standard Gaussian, which is where sampling starts.
